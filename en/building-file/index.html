What you get

The documents, and a guide to reading them

A Dutch building file is not one drawing. It is every permit ever granted for the address: the original construction, later extensions, a dormer window, sometimes the structural calculation. You receive all of it digitally.

With it comes a reader's guide of about one page, written in English:

  • What is in the file. Which years, which permit, and what is drawn on which sheet.
  • Where the structural information sits. The floor joists and their direction, the cross section, the foundation drawing, the steel beam above an opening.
  • What is missing. Archives have gaps. Knowing what you do not have is worth as much as knowing what you do.

One real example, from a terraced house built in 1963: four permits between 1963 and 1974, thirteen documents. The oldest permit covers the whole block of six houses and is filed under a street name that no longer exists. The document the archive calls a structural calculation opens with a list of twelve changes the municipality required before granting the permit, and those changes are what was actually built. None of that is visible to someone searching their own address.

Why this is harder than it looks

The archive does not know your address

Which archive holds the file depends on the year the permit was granted, not only on the municipality. One address can have three different counters: a regional archive for the oldest permits, the municipality for the middle decades, and a regional environmental service for anything recent.

On top of that, older files are often registered under the plan for an entire block, and under the street name as it was written at the time. Search your own address and you find nothing, and you conclude there is nothing.

And the archives work in Dutch. That is the part we take off your hands.
