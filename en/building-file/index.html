    An example

    What such a file looks like

    This is a real file, requested for a terraced house from 1963. Four permits, thirteen documents, twenty-eight pages, three of them structural calculations. On the right is the reader's guide I wrote to go with it.

    Three things stood in that guide, and they are exactly what you would not see yourself in that stack of scans:

    • The 1963 permit is not about this house but about the whole block of six, and it is filed under the street name as it was written then. That name has since changed. Search your own address and you find nothing.
    • The document the archive index calls a structural calculation opens with the municipality's list of objections: twelve points that had to be changed before the permit was granted. The drawing shows purlins of 8 by 18, that list says 8 by 20. What was built follows from the drawing plus the list.
    • What is not in it: the calculations of the concrete beams and lintels, one excepted, and nothing at all from after 1974.

    The guide describes what is there and what is missing. It does not judge. Which wall carries load, and what may come out, follows from a calculation on the measurements and the condition of the house as it stands now, not from old drawings.

    Why this is harder than it looks

    The archive does not know your address

    Which archive holds the file depends on the year the permit was granted, not only on the municipality. One address can have three counters: a regional archive for the oldest permits, the municipality for the middle decades, and a regional environmental service for anything recent. Municipalities have also merged. Search for Lekkerkerk and you end up in Krimpenerwaard, while the old files sit at the district archive in Gouda.

    That is why the tool above looks up the year your house was built, from the national register of addresses and buildings. That gives you the file of the original construction, which is usually what you want. For a later alteration, the year of that permit counts.

    Requesting it is not the hard part. An archive delivers what you name. Ask for the building drawing and you get the building drawing. The structural calculation is often a separate document under a different name, and the situation as altered sits in a file from twenty years later.

    And the archives work in Dutch. The search screens, the index terms and the documents themselves, including the handwriting on a 1930s drawing. That is the part I take off your hands.

    What goes into it:

    • The building file. Every permit granted at the address, from the original construction to the last alteration, with the drawings and, where they survive, the structural calculations.
    • The ground underneath. The cone penetration tests carried out nearby and the soil profile at the location, from the national subsurface register. That is the first thing an engineer looks up when a question touches the foundation.
    • The foundation picture of the neighbourhood. The risk of pile rot and of differential settlement according to the national climate atlas, with the caveat that those figures describe the neighbourhood and not your house.
    • The building itself. Year of construction, floor area, whether it stands in a row and how many homes share the building, from the national register of addresses and buildings. That last one often explains why the file covers a whole block.
    • The plot. The cadastral boundaries with the plot number, and whether the building has monument status.
    • The timeline. Aerial photographs year by year, so you can see when something changed, with the permits published about it. This is also where you check whether there are solar panels on the roof and since when: those rarely appear in a building file, while they do count for the roof structure.

    And two things that turn that stack into something usable:

    • The reader's guide, in English. What those documents say and where the structural information sits, in plain language.
    • The list of what is not there. This is the part you cannot produce yourself, because you only know what is missing once you know how such a file is supposed to be put together.
