Address report

What the records say about a house, before you bid

The Netherlands keeps a great deal of public data on every building: when it was built, what the ground underneath looks like, how likely pile rot is in the neighbourhood, which permits are in the archive and whether heritage rules apply. It sits in a dozen Dutch registers. I collect it for one address and explain it in English, with the source for each fact.

€ 95 per address, including VAT.

I am a structural engineer, and the report is written the way I would explain it to a client. It says what is known, what is not, and where the rest can be found.

An example

An apartment from 1886 in Amsterdam

This is a real report for a ground-floor apartment in Amsterdam Oud Zuid, three pages long.

The address, the building number, the parcel number and the numbers of the soil tests have been left out. Open the example as a PDF.

Among other things, the report for this address found:

  • The neighbourhood is one where 15% of the buildings are at risk of pile rot. Public data does not show whether this building's foundation has been investigated or repaired, so the report says which documents to ask the seller for.
  • The city archive holds six building files for the address. The ones from 2002 and 2003 contain structural calculations and a structural drawing, so structural work was done then. Those are the files to read before bidding.
  • The building lies in a protected cityscape the municipality designated in 2022. That matters if you want to change the windows or the roof.

What is in it

The building, the ground, the archive and the rules

  • The building. Year of construction, floor area, the number of homes in the building and the plot, from the national register of addresses and buildings and the land registry.
  • Foundation and ground. The risk of pile rot and subsidence in the neighbourhood according to the national climate atlas, and the soil layers from the nearest cone penetration tests in the national subsurface register. With an explanation of what this means for a building of that age, and of the foundation risk class your valuer will report.
  • Building files. Which archive holds the permit files for the address, per period. In Amsterdam the report also lists the files themselves: their year, what they contain, and whether structural calculations are among them.
  • Heritage and planning. Whether the building is a monument and whether it lies in a protected cityscape.
  • What is not in public data. Leasehold, the energy label, the owners' association and the WOZ value, with where to find each.

It does not contain an assessment of the building. The report describes what the sources say, and a figure for a neighbourhood says nothing certain about one house. The foundation risk class from the valuation report is not public, so the report cannot show it. Whether a foundation is sound takes a survey of the building itself.

Three levels

When the public records are not enough

The address report is the first level. The other two add what is in the archive, which takes a request to the municipality and some weeks.

Level 3

With the load-bearing structure

Levels 2 and 3 depend on the archive for your address delivering to me, which not every archive does. You check that on the building file page and order those levels there. If I do the structural calculation for the same address within twelve months, the amount for level 2 or 3 comes off it.
